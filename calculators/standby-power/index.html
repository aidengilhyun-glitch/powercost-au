What counts as standby power?

Standby power is electricity used when a device is not performing its main task but remains ready to respond. Common examples include televisions waiting for a remote signal, game consoles downloading updates, printers connected to Wi-Fi, microwave clocks, smart speakers, set-top boxes and computer equipment in sleep mode.

A single efficient device may use very little, but the combined cost can become noticeable when many devices remain connected every day.

Worked Australian example

Six devices drawing 5 watts each for 20 hours a day use 219 kWh a year. At 32 cents per kWh, that is about $70.08 per year. Replace the example values with your own tariff and a power-meter reading for a more useful estimate.

How to reduce standby use safely

  • Enable automatic sleep and energy-saving modes before unplugging equipment.
  • Switch off entertainment equipment at an accessible power board when it will not be used for an extended period.
  • Do not disconnect devices that need continuous power for safety, security, medical, recording or network functions.
  • Check whether consoles and computers use a higher-power instant-on mode.
  • Measure uncertain devices with a compliant plug-in electricity meter.

Frequently asked questions

Is standby power the same as an appliance being fully off?

No. Some products have a mechanical off switch and draw virtually nothing, while others remain connected to a power supply, network or sensor.

Does a smart plug use electricity?

Yes. A smart plug needs a small amount of power for its electronics and wireless connection. Include its own measured wattage when comparing savings.

Should I turn my fridge or router off?

Not as a routine standby-saving measure. Refrigeration, internet, alarms and other essential equipment may need continuous power. Focus on non-essential devices and follow manufacturer instructions.
