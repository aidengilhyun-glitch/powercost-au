Why self-consumed solar can be worth more

Each kilowatt-hour used directly may avoid buying one kilowatt-hour at your usage rate. An exported kilowatt-hour normally earns the lower feed-in tariff. For example, at a 32¢ usage rate and 5¢ feed-in tariff, shifting one kilowatt-hour from export to daytime household use changes its estimated value by 27¢.

This does not mean every load should be moved to daytime. Safety, comfort, appliance instructions and time-of-use rates still matter.

Worked example

A system generating 7,000 kWh a year with 35% used in the home supplies 2,450 kWh directly and exports 4,550 kWh. At 32¢/kWh for imported electricity and a 5¢/kWh feed-in tariff, the estimated annual value is $784 of avoided usage plus $227.50 of export credits, or $1,011.50 in total.

How to improve the estimate

  • Use a complete 12 months of inverter or retailer data when possible.
  • Enter the usage rate that applies when solar would otherwise be imported.
  • Check whether your feed-in tariff changes after an export threshold.
  • For time-of-use plans, calculate peak, shoulder and off-peak periods separately.
  • Do not treat this result as a quote for batteries, panels or installation work.

Frequently asked questions

Does this include the daily supply charge?

No. The supply charge usually remains payable regardless of solar generation, so this calculator focuses on variable electricity usage and export credits.

Does a battery automatically increase savings?

Not necessarily. A battery may shift solar energy into higher-value periods, but losses, capacity, cycle limits, tariffs, purchase price and usage patterns all affect the result. This calculator does not estimate battery payback.

Where can I find my self-consumption percentage?

Some inverter, battery and retailer portals show solar generation, household consumption and exports. Self-consumed energy is generation minus exported energy.
